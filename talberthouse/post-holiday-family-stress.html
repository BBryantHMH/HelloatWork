Holiday Family Stress: How to Go Home Without Falling Into Old Patterns

Bailey C. Bryant, Psy.D. · November 24, 2026 · Hello Mental Health

In the weeks before a holiday visit, a lot of people start playing things out in their heads. Who’s going to ask why you’re still single or when you’re having kids. What your uncle is going to say about politics this year. Or if you’re the parent, whether your adult kid will roll their eyes at dinner again, or whether that same old argument will come up before the pie is out. Psychologists call this anticipatory anxiety. It’s worry about something that hasn’t happened yet, and it often shows up as your mind rehearsing the worst version of what could possibly happen.

Holiday family stress usually isn’t about one big thing. It’s familiar patterns, old conflict, intrusive questions, offensive comments, and pressure to let boundaries slide. A lot of people also notice that once everyone’s under one roof, they act in ways they don’t usually act anywhere else. Adult kids get short with their parents or go quiet at the table. Parents slip back into managing and correcting. And everybody ends up in the same argument they’ve been having for twenty years.

There’s an old term for part of this. Sigmund Freud described regression as falling back on earlier, less mature ways of coping when we’re stressed, and Anna Freud later included it among the list of defense mechanisms we use to cope with life. It fits what a lot of people feel at the holidays.

One important note before going further: if someone in your family has been abusive or puts your safety at risk, protecting yourself comes first. Skipping the gathering, leaving early, or keeping your distance are all okay. A therapist can help you sort out what feels safe.

Why Holiday Family Stress Pulls Everyone Back Into Old Roles

Most holiday family stress comes from walking back into a family where everyone already has a part to play, and feeling pressure to play yours again. You can’t control how anyone else shows up, but you have more say over your own part than it probably feels like.

I’m not a family therapist, but I often use ideas from family systems work with individual clients. Psychiatrist Dr. Murray Bowen developed family systems theory, which views the family as one emotional unit rather than a collection of separate people. When one person changes, everyone else feels it.

Your family might still see the old you

The people who knew us when we were younger sometimes have a hard time updating their understanding of who we are now.

An adult kid might expect Mom to criticize, so they go in already defensive. A parent might still see their 34-year-old as the kid who forgot their homework and offer advice nobody asked for. Sometimes the expectation is fair. Mom might really criticize, and bracing for it makes sense. But on either side, expectations can also help the old pattern play out, whether we realize it or not. We end up reacting to who someone was ten years ago instead of who’s standing in the kitchen right now.

Families are also really good at keeping everyone in their predictable roles. Family systems folks call this homeostasis. When one person starts acting differently, the rest of the family often pushes to get things back to how they were. Usually that pull isn’t deliberate or even conscious. Families just tend to work that way.

Why It’s So Hard to Break the Pattern

You don’t have to play your old role, it’s just really hard not to. You can spend years in therapy or build a life you’re proud of, and still find yourself doing the old thing ten minutes after everyone’s together. That doesn’t mean you’ve failed. You’re stepping into a system that has run the same way for decades.

Bowen had a name for the work of staying yourself inside your family: differentiation of self. In his theory, it means being able to stay connected to your family while still holding on to your own thoughts, feelings, and choices. Most people keep working on this their whole lives.

One way to practice is to pick a moment where you usually fall into your role and try doing one thing differently:

  • If you’re the hero or perfectionist, maybe you let dinner be a little imperfect without jumping in to fix it.

  • If you’re the mascot or jokester, maybe you let a tense moment sit for a few seconds without cracking a joke.

  • If you’re the quiet one, maybe you share one real thing about your life instead of slipping off to hide.

  • If you’ve been cast as the scapegoat, maybe you don’t take the bait the first time someone pokes at you.

  • If you’re the peacemaker, maybe you resist the urge to smooth the tension after someone makes a sharp comment.

Then pay attention to what happens, both in the room and in you.

It probably won’t feel natural, and it won’t change everything this year. Patterns that took decades to form don’t shift over one dinner. Catching yourself partway through the old pattern still counts.

People can change, and so can you. Whether someone else changes is outside your control, and sometimes people don’t. Going in a little more curious about who they are now can still change how the day feels for you.

A Few Things That Actually Help

Decide your exit plan ahead of time. Know what time you’re leaving, or what you’ll do if things get heated, even if that’s just a walk around the block. Knowing you can leave often makes you feel less tense going in.

Pick one pattern to work on. Choose the moment you most want to handle differently. Trying to change everything at once is a lot to carry into one day.

Have a line ready for the question you know is coming. If someone always asks uncomfortable questions, decide in advance what you’ll say. Something short and true works, like “Nothing new to report, but I’ll let you know,” and then change the subject.

Plan some recovery time afterward. Build in a quiet evening, alone time with a good podcast, or a call with a friend who gets it. How you feel afterward also tells you something about what to try next time.

You Can Love Them and Still Dread It

There’s a lot of “and” at the holidays. You can love your family and still feel tense going into time with them. You can love the people who are there and still feel sad about the people who aren’t. You can love some parts of your family and really struggle with other parts.

Feeling more than one thing at once doesn’t make you ungrateful.

You Don’t Have to White-Knuckle It

You’re allowed to set limits and boundaries, even at the holidays, whether you’re the one visiting or the one hosting. The system will probably react. Someone might get quiet or irritated, or make a little comment. It can be really uncomfortable, and you can still be okay with it.

The way I see it, if you’re going to be uncomfortable anyway, it’s better to be uncomfortable about something you did that lines up with your values.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I act like a teenager when I go home for the holidays?

Stress and old surroundings can pull people back toward older ways of coping, which psychoanalytic theory calls regression. It’s really common and doesn’t mean you haven’t grown. Catching it while it’s happening is usually the first step toward doing something different.

What are family roles?

Family roles are patterns people take on to help a family function. Sharon Wegscheider-Cruse described roles like the hero, scapegoat, lost child, and mascot in families affected by alcoholism. They come from clinical observation rather than strong research evidence, but many people find them a helpful way to think about their own families.

How can parents make it easier when adult kids come home?

Try to treat your kids like the adults they are now, even when old habits kick in. Ask about their lives with curiosity, hold back advice unless they ask for it, and respect the limits they set, even if they’re new to you.

When should I talk to a therapist about family stress?

If the dread starts building weeks ahead, if visits leave you wiped out long after they end, or if you keep going back to the same old hurt, it might help to talk it through with someone. You can schedule a Mental Health Check-Up, find a provider, or take the free Self-Check. Family stress, boundaries, and grief are all things people work through in therapy all year long.

Before You Go

As you head into this season, see if you can soften a little. Be curious about who your family is now, and who you are now. If family stuff is still weighing on you after the holidays, help is here.
