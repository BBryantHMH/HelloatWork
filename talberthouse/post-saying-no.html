Boundaries

These two letters make a complete sentence: No.

Michelle Maegly, Psy.D. · October 1, 2026 · Hello Mental Health

When I was growing up, my mom had lots of self-helpy books on her shelf. The middle school me never picked them up, but some of the titles stuck in my brain. For the past few weeks, I’ve noticed a trend in sessions that keeps bringing one of them to mind: When I Say No, I Feel Guilty by Manuel J. Smith (1975). (This is not a book recommendation. I still haven’t actually read it.)

Why is it so hard to say “no”? That tiny, two-letter word can hold a lot of power in our lives, and yet we hesitate to use it all the time. So let’s talk about why saying “no” is hard, why it matters for your well-being, and how to do it.

Why Is Saying “No” So Hard?

It’s a very human dilemma, and one I fall into as much as the next person. A few of the most common reasons:

We want to be liked. Saying “yes” can feel like a way to avoid rejection or disapproval.

We like helping. Making other people happy feels good, so we agree even when it doesn’t fit our lives right now.

We want to avoid conflict. “Yes” can feel like the fastest way to keep the peace.

We feel guilty or obligated. We think we “should” say yes because it’s expected, even when it isn’t good for us.

We overestimate our capacity. We agree to one more thing, then another, and end up overwhelmed.

Culture and setting play a role. In some families, cultures, and workplaces, “no” can come across as rude or uncooperative. And if you never got to practice saying it, it makes sense that it feels awkward now.

The Freedom of “No”

Here’s the good news. Saying “no” when you need to can give you a lot back:

  • Time and energy for the people and commitments that matter most to you

  • Less stress, because you’re not spread so thin

  • Room for your relationships, since every “yes” to something less important takes time from someone who matters

  • More confidence, because each time you honor a limit, it gets a little easier to trust yourself

  • A good example for the people around you, who may need permission to set limits too

Saying “no” is really about making choices that line up with your well-being and values, so that your “yes” means something.

What About Saying “No” at Work?

At work, “no” isn’t always an option, and that’s real. Sometimes it sounds more like, “I can take that on, but something else will have to wait. Which is the priority?” Naming your limits and asking for help setting priorities is still a boundary, and in helping jobs it’s part of how you keep doing the work over the long haul.

How to Say “No” Kindly

  • Be clear and direct. A short, polite answer works better than a long, vague one.

  • You don’t owe a long explanation. A brief reason is fine if you want to give one. As the Mayo Clinic puts it, “no is a complete sentence.”

  • Offer an alternative if you want to. “I can’t do Friday, but I could help next week.”

  • Use “I” statements. “I can’t commit to this right now” lands better than “You’re asking too much.”

  • Give yourself time. If you’re unsure, it’s okay to say, “Let me think about it and get back to you.”

  • Be kind to yourself afterward. Guilt often shows up right after a “no,” and that doesn’t mean you did something wrong. The Self-Compassion Break can help in those moments.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it selfish to say no?
Setting limits is how you make sure you have something left to give. Most people respect a clear, kind “no” more than a reluctant “yes.”

Why do I feel guilty after saying no?
If you learned early that keeping others happy was your job, guilt can show up even when your “no” is completely reasonable. The feeling usually gets quieter the more you practice.

When is it worth talking to someone?
If saying “no” feels impossible, brings up intense fear, or keeps leading to burnout or resentment, a therapist can help you understand where that comes from and practice something different. You can find a provider through the library.

The next time you’re stuck between “yes” and “no,” remember that you get to choose what’s best for you. You might be surprised by what that small word makes room for.
